Signature Assignment

Prompt

Technofeminisms in Practice's Final Project

Throughout this course, we've asked how technology encodes assumptions about bodies, identities, and knowledge, and whose interests those assumptions serve. For your final project, you'll choose one of two tracks to put that thinking into practice: a Critical Platform Audit or a Feminist Platform Design. For the critical platform audit, students will select a real platform (such as a hiring platform, a social media app, a health tracker, etc.) and write a 4,000–5,000 word essay arguing what's at stake in its design by using the theoretical frameworks built this semester. If students would prefer to choose the second track, the feminist platform design track, they will build a small, functional prototype using Claude Code, and write a 2000–2,500 word document accounting for their design choices. Both tracks ask the same underlying questions of a concrete object, either a platform that already exists or one you build yourself: Who does this system serve? What does its infrastructure assume about the people who use it? What would it look like to do this differently?

Track A: Critical Platform Audit (Essay, 4,000–5,000 words)

Select a digital platform, a social media site, a health app, a hiring algorithm, a content moderation system, a recommender system, anything with real users and real design decisions behind it. Conduct a sustained feminist critical analysis of it.

Your audit should examine the platform's design choices, classification systems, data practices, and the assumptions about users built into its infrastructure. This is not a general summary of the platform or a list of its features. You are making an argument: what this system does, who it harms or erases, and what theoretical frameworks help us understand why.

A methodological note: one useful approach here is the walkthrough method (Light, Burgess, and Duguay, 2018), which treats close, systematic engagement with an app's interface, onboarding flow, and everyday functions as a legitimate research method in its own right. You don't have to use this method, but it's a good starting point if you're not sure how to structure your close analysis of a platform you don't have insider access to.

If you use AI tools anywhere in your drafting or research process for this track, disclosure is required (see AI Use guidelines below), but using AI tools is not required for this track.

Track B: Feminist Platform Design (Prototype + Design Document, 2,500–3,000 words)

Build a small, functional prototype of a platform or tool, using Claude Code (skills developed in our in-class workshop), grounded in the theoretical commitments we've built across the semester. Your prototype doesn't need to be polished or production-ready. It needs to embody a clear argument about what a platform would look like if it took seriously the critiques we've spent the semester developing.

Alongside your prototype, submit a design document that explains:

  • The problem or gap you identified, and why it matters
  • The specific design choices you made and the theoretical commitments behind them
  • What Claude Code made possible, and where it fell short or produced results you had to fight against or work around

Using Claude Code is required for this track. This isn't a technical proficiency requirement so much as an experiential one: I want you to have actually worked with an AI coding tool so you can write about it with specificity, not from secondhand impressions.

Outcomes It Maps To

By the end of this project, you will be able to:

  • Conduct a sustained, theoretically grounded critical analysis of a digital platform or system by using the feminist STS and critical data studies frameworks developed across the semester.
  • Name and defend the central arguments of the major scholars anchoring this course (Haraway, Noble, Benjamin, Costanza-Chock, and others) and apply their ideas to a case you choose yourself.
  • Articulate your own position on the relationship between feminism and technology, which is distinct from summarizing what others have argued.
  • Demonstrate critical AI literacy, including a working understanding of what an AI tool can and cannot do, which is gained through direct use.
Sequence & Scaffolding

This is the final project for Technofeminisms in Practice, a graduate seminar in the WGSS MA program. Students arrive at this assignment having already done preparatory work earlier in the class. Firstly, they have engaged with feminist theories in the discussion posts written throughout the semester. Specifically, students worked through Haraway's A Cyborg Manifesto, Noble's Algorithms of Oppression, Benjamin's Race After Technology, and Costanza-Chock's design justice framework. Students can and should draw on ideas and positions they developed by reading these works. This assignment asks them to deepen and apply that thinking further. Students have also learned the mechanics of how to use Claude Code in an earlier workshop on the topic. They got hands-on instruction using AI coding and developed a basic project already. What is new for this assignment is the application of both their thoughts on theory and the application of that combined with technology.

Rubric

Theoretical engagement (30%)

Does the work demonstrate an accurate and specific understanding of at least three scholars and/or frameworks from this course? Are concepts (like the cyborg, algorithmic oppression, design justice, and so on) applied precisely to your chosen case, not just gestured at?

Argument and personal position (35%)

Is there a clear, defensible argument, not just a survey of relevant theory? Can you articulate your own position on the feminism-technology relationship, distinct from summarizing what Haraway or Noble or Benjamin already said? Knowing what a scholar argues is necessary, but it isn't the same as knowing what you think, and I'm assessing both separately on purpose.

Critical AI literacy (20%)

For Track B: does the design document demonstrate genuine, specific understanding of what Claude Code did and didn't do well, not just a claim that it "worked" or "didn't work"? For Track A: if AI tools were used, is the disclosure honest and specific? Did the reflection engage with how the platform is or is not using AI? Does it discuss the author's own vantage point of what AI is or is not doing for this platform?

Reflection document (15%)

Does the reflection make your intellectual labor visible? What did you change your mind about? Where does your position sit in relation to the scholars we've read? What did the process actually teach you, including the parts that didn't go smoothly?

Sample Artifact

For Track A

Here is a mock excerpt, so you can see the tone and depth I'm looking for:

Fertility and period-tracking apps like Flo and Clue promise users a kind of intimate self-knowledge: know your body, predict your cycle, plan your life around it. But the classification systems underneath these apps quietly narrow who counts as a legitimate user. The onboarding flow assumes a goal (pregnancy or avoiding it), a body (cisgender, with a uterus, tracking toward reproduction), and a relationship to that body (voluntary, ongoing disclosure). Someone tracking dysphoria alongside their cycle, or tracking for medical reasons unrelated to fertility, finds no category that fits. This isn't a minor UX oversight. It's what Bowker and Star would call the residual category problem: the app's data model has already decided who is default and who is exception, and that decision shapes what the app can see, predict, or protect.

Notice the moves: name the platform specifically, describe an actual design choice, connect it to a named theoretical concept, and say what's at stake. However, it's important this is an example of tone, not length.

For Track B

Here is a mock excerpt, so you can see the tone and depth I'm looking for:

Callisto is a real nonprofit platform built for survivors of sexual assault to document incidents and, if they choose, discover whether others have been harmed by the same person, without being forced into a formal report. It's a useful aspirational model for a few reasons. It's small and specific rather than trying to solve everything at once. Its design choices are directly traceable to a theoretical and political commitment (trauma-informed design, survivor autonomy, refusing to force a binary between reporting and not reporting). And in a later redesign, its team explicitly rebuilt parts of the interface to stop assuming a gender binary, replacing pronoun selectors with open text fields specifically to be more inclusive of LGBTQ survivors. That's the kind of traceable line from theoretical commitment to interface decision I want to see in your design document, even at a much smaller scale.

Your prototype will have the same kind of traceability: I should be able to read your design document and see exactly which design choice answers which critique from our readings. However, it's important this is an example of tone, not length.

Student Reflection

Track A (Critical Platform Audit): Using AI tools is optional. If you use any AI tool anywhere in your process, research, drafting, or editing, you must disclose it in your reflection document. I'm not asking for prompt logs or screenshots. I want a narrative account: what you used the tool for, what it got right, what it got wrong or missed, and how that shaped your final work. If you didn't use AI tools at all, say so; that's a legitimate answer too.

Track B (Feminist Platform Design): Using Claude Code is required. Your design document must include a substantive narrative reflection on that experience. I want you to address, at minimum:

  • What you asked Claude Code to help you build, and how you approached that
  • Where it produced something useful or unexpectedly good
  • Where it failed, produced something you had to discard, or revealed an assumption baked into the tool itself that you had to design around
  • Whether anything about the experience changed how you think about AI tools in relation to the critiques we've read this semester

No screenshots or prompt logs are required for either track. This is a deliberate choice: I'm less interested in auditing your keystrokes than in whether you can think and write critically about your own process. A narrative reflection asks more of you, not less, because you can't hide behind a transcript. You have to actually make sense of what happened.
