Research Statement

Humanistic inquiry drives my engagement with scientific and technical communication practices, particularly data infrastructures that determine who becomes visible to care, funding, and protection. Working at the intersection of critical data studies, feminist technoscience, and information visualization, I study how classification systems encode social location, especially gender and sexuality, and what happens to public knowledge when those systems are rewritten. I approach documentation as an active instrument rather than a passive record. Federal health surveillance forms, refugee intake questionnaires, and platform disclosure fields participate in producing the categories they claim only to describe, and those categories carry material consequences for the people sorted by them. This orientation draws on Haraway’s situated knowledges, Bowker and Star’s work on classification and infrastructure, Spade’s account of administrative violence, and Costanza-Chock’s design justice, and it commits me to research conducted with the communities whose data is at stake rather than about them.

My dissertation, Erased by Design: What Happens When the State Stops Counting Gender, and Who Is Counting Instead, examines the replacement of “gender” with “sex” across federal health data infrastructure following Executive Order 14168, focusing on CDC surveillance systems and on the counter-data practices communities have built in response. The project makes two central claims. First, sex and gender are distinct and non-interchangeable concepts, so substituting one for the other degrades both current and future knowledge, narrowing what can be asked about disease in general rather than only what can be known about trans populations. Second, surveillance that counts the most marginalized groups, including trans people of color, improves health knowledge for the whole population, which means sidelining them is a loss borne by everyone. I treat the instruments themselves as objects of technical and professional communication rather than as neutral background to policy change.

My work as a Graduate Research Associate at the Center for Humanities and Digital Research has grounded that commitment in collaborative practice, including an immersive installation documenting state-sponsored and independent Cuban artists, and earlier NSF-supported collaborative research on histories of racialized violence in Polk County, Florida, where I developed the concept of “atmospheres of white memory” to describe how digital representations of those geographies are whitened in the telling.

My most recent publications extend this analysis across the settings where classification does its work. A forthcoming chapter for the Oxford Handbook on AI and the Future of Social Welfare examines trans and nonbinary refugee resettlement, predictive welfare systems, and the binary data infrastructures that route people through them. An upcoming chapter for Intimate Technologies of Reproductive Health: Interdisciplinary Perspectives highlights an article I developed on what period tracking apps do to trans masculine users who are simultaneously excluded from the imagined user and included in its data extraction. This work traces how cisnormative design logics shape affective relations to the body, commodify intimate disclosure, and foreclose the embodied knowledge practices trans masculine creators have developed in their place.

My most recent and current research centers the relationship between intimacy, bodies, and technology, asking how platforms that promise connection simultaneously surveil, misrecognize, and expose the people who use them. This work reconceives everyday behaviors like blocking, pausing, and location withholding as protective refusal directed at other users and the platform at once, a reframing grounded in feminist and trans technoscience. My methodology, combining public-facing discourse analysis with structured walkthroughs of platform affordances, reflects a deliberate choice to work with what users have chosen to make public rather than extracting from private community spaces.

Alongside my written scholarship, I build. My training in digital humanities methods informs both how I analyze infrastructures and what I make in response to them: a counter-map of racial violence in Polk County built in Leaflet.js, a distant reading of Technical Communication Quarterly using Voyant Tools alongside large language models as parallel methods, generative work in p5.js and Tracery, and a searchable archive and concept map developed as a public teaching resource. I treat visualization as an argumentative form rather than an illustration of findings established elsewhere, and I am interested in what becomes arguable when absence itself is rendered legible.

My emerging book-length project takes dating apps as its primary site for tracing how intimate harm operates as a structure across scales, from the interpersonal friction of a blocked profile or a deadnamed interface, to the platform logics that classify and extract from trans and marginalized users while refusing to see them, to the federal data infrastructures that perform the same erasure at the population level. Drawing on feminist and trans technoscience, the project argues that intimacy is not only a property of close relationships but a condition of vulnerability that technologies exploit whenever they promise recognition and deliver misrecognition instead.
